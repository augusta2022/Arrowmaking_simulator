# Arrow-making simulator

A teaching activity on how craft production was organised in the Qin workshops that armed
the Terracotta Army. Students cast bronze, assemble arrows and tie bundles under two
organisational models, then read the chemical pattern each one leaves behind.

After Martinón-Torres, M., Li, X. J., Bevan, A., Xia, Y., Zhao, K. & Rehren, Th.,
“Forty Thousand Arms for a Single Emperor: From Chemical Data to the Labor Organization
Behind the Bronze Arrows of the Terracotta Army,” *Journal of Archaeological Method and
Theory* 21 (2014): 534–562. doi:10.1007/s10816-012-9158-z

## The activity

**1 · Flow line** — Six specialised workshops each pour one crucible load. Finished trays go
to a shared stock bin, where the pieces are shuffled and face-down. Assembly stays locked
until every workshop has poured and every tray is in the store, so arrows are always built
from a fully mixed stockpile.

**2 · Cellular** — Three self-contained cells. Each pours its own heads and its own tangs,
assembles its own arrows and ties its own bundle, and cannot pour again until that bundle is
finished.

**3 · Arrow chemistry** — The bundles from both runs, as colour grids and as lead-against-tin
scatter plots. Both systems draw on the same six crucible loads, so any difference between
them comes from the order of operations alone.

Colour stands for a crucible load; in the plots, position carries the chemistry and colour
and symbol carry the bundle.


## Notes on the model

Scale is compressed throughout so a class can work both systems in a few minutes. Crucible
loads are separated here by tin and lead plotted against each other, as in the study; the real
analyses draw further support from minor elements such as antimony and arsenic, and because
unprepared, corroded surfaces had to be measured, the published values are read as trends
rather than absolute weight percentages.

Because the flow-line draw is blind, a bundle will occasionally come out with four of its five
heads from one melt by chance. That is sampling variation, not a fault — and a useful thing to
discuss.
